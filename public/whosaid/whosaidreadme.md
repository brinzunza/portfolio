Subtitles tell you what was said, but never who said it. Watch three people talk over each other with a single line of text at the bottom of the screen and you spend half of your attention deciphering. It gets worse for the content you rely on subtitles for the most: anything in a language you do not speak. I have always had this problem, and what bothers me is that the text lives at the very bottom of the screen. Every time I look down to read, I miss the picture. The glance across the table, the reaction, the thing someone is holding up. I want to be watching more and reading less.

Captions were designed for a time when putting a line of text at the bottom was the best anyone could do. With how far audio and vision models have come, it is hard not to try something better. So I built [__whosaid__](https://github.com/brinzunza/whosaid): captions that appear directly under the face of whoever is speaking, and follow that person around the frame as they move. Your eyes stay on the people, and the words arrive where the voice comes from.

The idea is easy to say and surprisingly hard to do. It breaks into three jobs: hearing what was said, working out who said it, and putting the words in the right place at the right moment.

**Hearing** is the part that listens and returns accurate text, in any language. The difficulty is everything real people do to speech. They have accents, they mumble, they talk fast, they talk over each other, and there is music and a crowd behind them. The overlap is the one that matters most. When two people speak at once, a normal transcription gives you a blended mess that belongs to nobody, so the voices have to be pulled apart before anything can be written down.

**Knowing who** is the part that decides which words belong to which person. It sounds like it should be easy, since you can see who is talking. But a moving mouth is not the same as speech. Someone laughing, chewing, or silently mouthing along looks a lot like someone talking, and the only reliable way to tell the difference is to check that the lips and the sound actually agree with each other, which is how we do it as humans without thinking.

**Placing** is the part that follows the speaker and puts the text under them smoothly, at the time they are talking. This is where the edge cases live. People speak while off screen. The camera cuts to a close-up of someone new, and the system has to understand that it is a new person and not a continuation of the last one. Someone walks out of the frame mid-sentence. In those cases the caption should not teleport or vanish. It slides to the edge the person left through and stays there until they are back.

What I care about most is what this is actually useful for. The obvious one is foreign-language film and television. Reading subtitles in a language you do not speak is the case where the bottom-of-the-screen problem is the worst, because you are already working hard to follow the story. Putting the translation under the speaker lets you keep your eyes on the performance, which in a foreign film is half of what you are there for.

The accessibility case may be the most important. For deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers, group conversations are the hardest content to follow, precisely because standard captions rarely say who is talking, and keeping up with a fast back-and-forth means constantly guessing. Captions that point at the speaker take away most of that guesswork.

Beyond that, it applies anywhere there are multiple people on camera and someone wants to read along: interviews, panel discussions, debates, documentaries, lectures with audience questions, recorded meetings, and the short vertical videos that already rely on captions to be watched on mute. For anyone who makes videos, it would remove the tedious manual work of placing and timing captions around who is on screen.
